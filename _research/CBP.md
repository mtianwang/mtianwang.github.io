Circumbinary planets are planets that orbit around a pair of stars rather than a single star.  
A familiar fictional example is Tatooine in *Star Wars*: from the surface of such a planet, one would see two suns in the sky.

Observed transiting circumbinary planets are typically found just outside the dynamical instability region of the central binary.  
In other words, they orbit very close to the innermost stable location allowed by the gravity of the two stars. If they were only slightly closer to the binary, their orbits would become unstable.

This orbital architecture provides an important clue to their formation. The conventional picture is that circumbinary planets are difficult to form *in situ* near their current locations, because the inner region of a circumbinary disk is strongly perturbed by the central binary. The disk can become eccentric, dynamically hot, and hostile to efficient planet growth. A more natural scenario is that these planets formed farther out in the disk and later migrated inward, stopping near the inner disk edge and the stability boundary.

Another striking property of the known transiting circumbinary planets is their strong coplanarity: most of them are aligned with the orbital plane of the central binary to within only a few degrees. However, circumbinary disks themselves can have a much wider range of orientations, including highly misaligned or even polar configurations. If planets can form in such disks, then some circumbinary planets may also occupy exotic, non-coplanar orbits. Finding and characterizing such systems is therefore an important way to test how circumbinary planets form and evolve.

This motivated us to revisit **TOI-1338**, the first circumbinary planet system discovered by TESS. The system contains a transiting, nearly coplanar planet, **TOI-1338 b**, and an outer planet detected through radial velocities, **TOI-1338 c**, whose orbital inclination was not directly known. This raised a natural question:

> **Could the outer planet be a misaligned, or even polar, circumbinary planet?**

To answer this question, we combined TESS photometry of the planetary transits and stellar eclipses with published HARPS and ESPRESSO radial velocities in a global photodynamical model. Unlike a standard Keplerian fit, this approach self-consistently follows the gravitational interactions among the two stars and the two planets, allowing the transits, eclipses, radial velocities, and dynamical perturbations to be modeled together.

We found that **TOI-1338 is a highly coplanar circumbinary multi-planet system**. Both planets are closely aligned with the orbital plane of the central binary, ruling out a polar configuration for the outer planet. This architecture points to a dynamically quiet formation history, in which the planets likely formed within a single, flat circumbinary disk and avoided strong later perturbations.

Our analysis also demonstrates the power of photodynamical modeling for revealing the three-dimensional architecture of circumbinary systems. Although TOI-1338 c does not transit, its orbit can still be constrained through the gravitational perturbations it induces on the transits of TOI-1338 b, together with the radial-velocity signal. In this way, a non-transiting planet can still leave a measurable dynamical imprint on the system.

Finally, **TOI-1338 b is an unusually low-density, gas-rich planet**. Its puffy nature suggests that it may have accreted a substantial gaseous envelope in a cooler outer region of the circumbinary disk before migrating inward to its current orbit. Together, the coplanar architecture and the low density of TOI-1338 b support the standard picture in which circumbinary planets form farther out in the disk and migrate inward to the edge of the stable region.
