Modern planet formation theory suggests that planets typically form in a protoplanetary disk, where differential migration naturally drives multi-planet systems into tightly packed, resonant configurations known as mean-motion resonances (MMRs). 

However, observations of mature planetary systems from missions like Kepler present a long-standing puzzle: only a tiny fraction of these systems (~15%) remain locked in resonances today. To resolve this discrepancy, the "breaking the chain" model proposes that nearly all compact planetary systems are born in pristine resonant chains, but the vast majority (~85%) eventually become dynamically unstable after the gas disk disperses. This transition leads to a phase of close encounters and late-stage rearrangement that permanently disrupts their original resonant architecture. 

Recent population-level studies provide critical empirical support for this evolutionary pathway, revealing a clear trend where the fraction of resonant configurations steadily declines with age—dropping from about 80% in systems younger than 100 Myr, down to 30% in adolescent systems (0.1–1 Gyr), and finally settling at 15% for mature ones (>1 Gyr). 

 Our research provides a unique and direct observational anchor for this transition framework by offering a detailed characterization of the ~200-Myr-old TOI-2076 system, a crucial adolescent signpost hosting four sub-Neptune planets. By performing a joint photodynamical analysis of transit-timing variations (TTVs) and radial velocity (RV) data to break mass-eccentricity degeneracies, 
 
 we mapped the system's orbital solutions onto the analytical Hamiltonian framework of resonant dynamics. We discovered that the planets in TOI-2076 are near-resonant but firmly out of true resonance, occupying a dynamically fragile state where their resonance angles circulate freely rather than librate. This architecture directly catches a compact system in the intermediate stage of "breaking its chains," diverging from the pristine configuration expected from disk migration.

 Furthermore, we found a monotonic increase in H/He envelope mass fractions with increasing distance from the star, demonstrating that intense XUV-driven photoevaporation is actively stripping the inner worlds down to rocky cores while less-irradiated outer planets retain primordial atmospheres. The planet b is currently hosting an envelope 1% of total mass, which is a predicted outcome for photoevaporation, which predicts a planet either loses all it primordial envelope, or eventually cling to a envelope fraction of 1%. 
 
 TOI-2076 thus serves as a benchmark proving that both the orbital and atmospheric reshaping of compact planetary systems begin early and operate in tandem to sculpt the final architectures we see today.
